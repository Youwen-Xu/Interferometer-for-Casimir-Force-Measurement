              on fringe-pattern analysis. Characteristics of the spatial-carrier technique and the temporal-
              carrier technique are compared, and the relationship between the two techniques is clarifed.
              Recent developments in the spatial-carrier fringe-pattern analysis techniques and their applica-
              tion areas are reviewed.

              transform.

           2. Spatial-carrier technique                                   
                                                                          
            In this section, we first explain how the spatial-carrier technique is related to
           the temporal-carrier technique, and then describe its advantages and disadvantages
           relative to the temporal-carrier technique.                    
            A common way of generating the spatial-carrier frequency is to give a relatively
           large tilt to the reference mirror as shown in Fig. 2 (left). Let us focus our attention
           on a small area of the interferogram which extends just over one period of the
           spatial-carrier frequency. The middle part of Fig. 2 shows a magnification of the
           corresponding part of the fringe and the tilted mirror. Suppose we sample this
           fringe at M points and consider the optical path lengths to and from the tilted
           mirror at each sample point. Then we note that the role played by the tilted mirror
           is equivalent to that played by multiple mirror segments each of which is shifted
           by 2/2M relative to its neighbors, as shown in Fig. 2 (right). Provided that the
           phase and the amplitude of the wave to be measured do not change appreciably
           within the period of the spatial-carrier frequency, such a tilted mirror system works
           as a multi-channel parallel phase-shift interferometer, in which all the phase shifts
           are encoded simultaneously on the spatial position in the single interferogram
           rather than sequentially on multiple time-stamped interferograms obtained by
           scanning a movable mirror. This spatial parallelism is an essential feature of the
           spatial-carrier technique, which makes possible the heterodyne interferometric
           measurement of very fast phenomena.                            
            Relative advantages and disadvantages of the spatial-carrier technique in com-
           parison with the temporal-carrier technique may be summarized as follows:
                                                                          
           Advantages                                                     
           (1)  Only one interferogram is needed while the temporal-carrier technique
                requires multiple interferograms taken at different times.
           (2)  No special devices are needed for carrier generation whereas the temporal-
                carrier technique requires a frequency- or phase-shifting device.

           Disadvantages                                                  
           (1)  The detector array must have a higher spatial resolution than in the tempo-

           (2)  The spatial distribution of detector sensitivities must be uniform over the
                array.                                                    
                                                                          
            Clearly, the requirements on the detector array are more stringent in the spatial-
           carrier technique. This may have limited the usefulness of the technique at the time
           when such a detector array was not available, but the recent rapid development
           in fabrication technology for high-resolution image sensors seems to be changing
           the situation more in favor of the spatial-carrier technique.  
                                                                          
           2. Spatial-carrier fringe-pattern analysis                     
                                                                          
            The principal problem in (cid:127)nge-pattern analysis is how to obtain the phase
           information ~b(x, y) from a given fringe pattern, by separating it from the other
           terms a(x, y) and b(x, y). This can be done rather easily in the temporal-carrier
           technique, since it employs time t as an extra parameter so that the problem is
           reduced to reading the phase of a pure sinusoidal signal on the time coordinate,
           with the space coordinates fixed at the point to be probed. The spatial-carrier
           technique requires slightly more sophisticated processing since all the signals are
           mixed up in the same space coordinates.

           2.2. Space-domain processing (sinusoid convolution)            
                                                                          
            A form of processing which is equivalent to the Fourier transform method can
           be performed in the space signal domain. From eqn. (6), the spectrum passed by
           the filter function H(fx -fo, Y) is written as                 
                                                                          
                C(f~-fo, Y)= H(fx-fo, y)G(f~, y).             (9)         
           By taking the inverse Fourier transform of eqn. (9), the equivalent processing in
           space domain is represented by                                 
                                                                          
                c(x, y) exp (2nifox) = h(x, y) exp (2nifox)*g(x, y). (10) 
           where • denotes the convolution operation, and h(x, y) is an impulse response
           function defined by the inverse Fourier transform of H(fx, y) with respect to fx.
           The sinusoid convolution of eqn. (10) was proposed by Mertz 10, and its relation
           to the frequency domain analysis was clarified by Womack 11. When we compute
           the phase from eqn. (10) by using (7) or (8), we need to subtract the linear phase
           introduced by the spatial-carrier frequency. As in the Fourier transform method,
           the carrier frequency can be removed by translating the spectrum, eqn. (9), in the
           frequency domain, so that                                      
                                                                          
                C(f~, y) = H(f~, y)G(f~ +fo, Y).              (11)        
           The corresponding space-domain processing can be expressed by  
                                                                          
                c(x, y)= h(x, y).g(x, y) exp (-2rdfoX).       (12)

           2.3. Computational complexities                                
                                                                          
            Suppose the one-dimensional interferogram is sampled at N points and pro-
           cessed by a digital computer. The number of operations (multiplications and addi-
           tions) that must be performed in the Fourier transform method is of the order of
           N log N, provided that an FFT algorithm is used. In the sinusoid convolution in
           space domain, the corresponding number of operation is of the order of raN, where
           m is the number of sample points included in the convolution kernel or the spread
           of the impulse response h(x, y); m takes its minimum value 3, when we choose the
           spread of a boxcar impulse response equal to one period of the carrier frequency,
           Ax = lifo. It is clear that the method of sinusoid convolution in space domain
           has a significant computational advantage over the Fourier transform method.
           However, it should be noted that this computational efficiency is gained at the
           cost of accuracy since the filter function as shown in Fig. 10 is not an ideal one.
           To realize an ideal narrow-band bandpass filter, such as shown in Fig. 3, in space
           domain, we need to convolve an impulse response function that has a large spread,
           which requires m to be as large as log N.            3.2. Influence of noise                                        
                                                                          
            For a small additive white noise, rms phase errors in heterodyne techniques can
           be related to signal-to-noise ratios (SNR) by relatively simple formulas. In the case
           of the temporal-carrier technique, Bruning [22] has shown that the rms phase
           error for the fringe scanning method is given by               
                x//~[Aq~(x, y)]2) = (1/N)I/2/S(x, y).            where n is the number of spectral sample points within the filter passband in the
           frequency domain. A similar formula was given for the two-dimensional case by
           Bone et al. 7. These results can be interpreted as follows. Since in the temporal-
           carrier technique the phase is obtained from the single-frequency component of a
           pure sinusoid plus white noise, only 1/N of the total noise power is included in
           the filtered spectrum, so that we have the factor (l/N) i/2 in the phase error in
           eqn. (19). In the Fourier transform method, on the other hand, the phase is obtained
           from the multiple, n, frequency spectra spreading over the filter passband. The
           spectra passed by the filter, therefore, include n/N of the total noise power, and
           under the small-noise assumption the phase error is proportional to the factor
           (n/N). A more detailed comparison of the noise characteristics between the tempo-
           ral-carrier technique and the spatial-carrier technique was given by Takeda 24.
                                                                          
           3.3. Detector nonlinearity                                     
                                                                          
            Generally, the Fourier transform method is relatively robust to detector non-
           linearity as far as the higher harmonics generated by the nonlinearity are weak
           and/or can be separated from the signal on the fundamental carrier frequency.
           However, large nonlinearity causes a significant error. This issue was addressed
          and its correction algorithm proposed by Nugent 25.

            The idea of holographic interferometry can be extended to the use of electron
           waves. The phase difference between two electron beams is proportional to the
           magnetic flux enclosed by the two beams and is given by the formula of the
           Aharonov-Bohm effect,                                          
                                                                          
                A49 = - (2~e/h) l Bn dS,                     (22)         
           where e is the electron charge, h is Planck's constant, and B is the magnetic flux
           density. Tonomura et al. 30 have developed an electron holography microscope
           and experimentally verified this Aharonov-Bohm effect. According to eqn. (21),
           one fringe in an electron wave interferogram corresponds to an enclosed magnetic
           flux of h/e =4.1 x 10 -aS Wb, which represents a very high sensitivity compared
           with that of any other conventional field-measurement technique. With the collab-
           oration of Tonomura and Endo, Takeda and Ru 31 applied the Fourier trans-
           form method to the analysis of electron-wave interferograms, and demonstrated
           the measurement of extremely small magnetic fields leaked out of a magnetic film
           on which a high-density magnetic bit pattern was recorded with a bit length of
           0.15 p.m. Figure 15 shows an example of the measured electron phase distribution.
           The contour lines of the phase represent the force lines of the stray magnetic field
           leaked from the segment of the cobalt film corresponding to a 1-bit recording.
           Since there is no ambiguity as to hills and valleys of the measured electron wave
           front, directions of the magnetizations and force lines can be uniquely determined.
